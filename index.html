Hello, this is my portfolio: a selection of things I have done, and the place where I will post updates on my current work.

I am an Amsterdam-based physicist, artist and science communicator. During my PhD I studied how light and motion interact at the smallest scales. In my artistic practice I carry that experimental approach out of the lab and into performance and installation, where light is transduced to sound and the experiment expands to meet perception and emotion. At the University of Amsterdam I work between both worlds: I write about quantum research, bring artists and quantum scientists together, and look for ways to talk about what this technology might mean for society.

Science communication

University of Amsterdam, now

At the University of Amsterdam I am science communication advisor for QuSoft, the Dutch research centre for quantum software. There I am immersed in academic research in mathematics, theoretical physics and computer science. I write articles that help disseminate the academic research and dedicate myself to outreach projects such as the Quantum Experience. I am also project and content lead for QDNL, the national quantum programme. My role is setting up collaborations between artists and quantum scientists. Broadly, I look for ways to talk about what this technology might mean for society, beyond the hype.

02 · λ 589 nm

Art

Sandberg Institute MFA 2023 to 2025 · Postnatural Independent Programme 2026 · now

My artistic practice allows me to reinvent the experimental approach I cultivated as a physicist in the lab. I am particularly drawn to concepts and ideas that belong to philosophy and social science. Therefore the practice is constantly negotiating with academic research in these fields. In Reality comes in waves I show research through performance and installation: light is transduced to sound. Central, however, is the dialogue between the performative gesture and the listener. It is there that the physics experiment expands to meet perception and emotion. I am interested in the concept of individuation (derived from Simondon), as constant transformation, in the material and immaterial (re-)configurations that are mediated by technical objects. I did my MFA at the Sandberg Institute in Amsterdam (Artificial Times, 2023 to 2025), graduating with Reality comes in waves, and in 2026 joined the Postnatural Independent Programme of the Institute of Postnatural Studies in Madrid.

03 · λ 532 nm

Physics

PhD 2016 to 2020 · Postdoc 2021 to 2022

How do you look at something without disturbing it? Close to the quantum regime, every measurement changes what it measures. My PhD at AMOLF, in the Photonic Forces group, worked near that limit: I built tiny vibrating beams, used laser light to measure them, and tried to quickly glance at their motion so that it wouldn't be disturbed. Even thermal noise became a signal, written into the light that left them. During a one-year postdoctoral fellowship at the Italian Institute of Technology (2021 to 2022), I contributed to the development of nanopore sensing technologies exploiting anti-Stokes Raman scattering, aimed at sequencing single proteins.

About the front page

The top of this page is a small drawing of quantum waves you can touch.

What you see

Each blob of light is the wave of a cold atom, sitting in its own tiny trap. Together they behave as one wave spread over all the traps, so neighbours can interfere. The traps sit on a quasicrystal: a pattern with five-fold symmetry that is ordered but never repeats, famous for its five-fold diffraction. Its points are the corners of a Penrose tiling, the pattern Roger Penrose found in the 1970s: two kinds of rhombus that cover the plane without ever repeating.

When the page opens the atoms are hot. A hot atom is a mixture of many states, a pale blur without a definite phase. As they cool, a growing part of each settles into one coherent wave, and colour appears: it shows the phase of the wave. Once they are cold, moving the cursormoving your finger kicks them: the waves near it get a push, tilt into stripes and interfere with their neighbours, and the motion spreads to the waves around them before they settle again. Everything you do is mirrored five times around the centre.

The physics

A hot atom is a thermal mixture: a statistical mix of many waves with random phases, so it is not a single wave with a phase. As it cools, a coherent fraction f grows, until the mixture becomes a pure state, which has a phase. Even then the wave keeps a width σ₀, because a quantum particle cannot sit still at a single point. Brightness shows where an atom is likely to be found, |Ψ|²; colour shows the phase of its wave, arg Ψ.

n(r) = f |Ψc(r)|² + (1−f) Σj gT(r−rj)  ·  f = 1 − (T/Tc)²
Ψ(r,t) = Σj e−|r−rj|²/2σ² ei(k·(r−rj) − ωt)
ΔE = ħΩ [ (σ/σ0)² + (σ0/σ)² + (kσ0)² ] / 2 − ħΩ

This is a drawing made with the equations of quantum mechanics, not a simulation: the formulas are real, the timing is choreographed. The cooling follows a prescribed curve, the settling after a kick is a smooth fade, and the fraction f is borrowed from Bose–Einstein condensation, where many atoms share one wave. Interference happens with light and water too. The more specifically quantum parts are reading the wave as a probability, the width that remains at zero temperature, and the difference between a mixture and a pure state.

Below are three smaller versions of the experiment, each showing one idea on its own. Move the cursor over themTouch them, or press restart.

Heat

The atoms arrive hot and cool down, as at the top of the page, and the cursoryour finger heats them up again: they turn back into a pale mixture without a phase. The heat spreads to their neighbours, and when you stop they cool down again.

Kick

No temperature at all: every atom starts in its lowest-energy state, a pure wave at rest. The cursorYour finger kicks them, and ΔE shows the energy of the most excited one.

Release

The traps switch off. Each wave spreads freely, overlaps its neighbours and interferes with them. Then the expansion runs backwards and the waves refocus into their traps. The cursorYour finger releases the atoms near it.

Running the expansion backwards stands in for what a lab would do: switch the traps back on and cool. Spreading waves get fainter, so the brightness is rescaled, like a camera's auto-exposure. The same formula also describes a laser beam spreading out.
